The alert fires

Amara meets a real AI tool. Read the scene, look at what the alert says, then open each of the three responses. There is no score and nothing you choose is recorded.

The scene

Wednesday, 14:10

Three weeks ago, the hospital switched on a deterioration predictor. It was built with supervised machine learning: nobody wrote its rules by hand. It was trained on thousands of past admissions where staff already knew who went on to become more unwell, and it learned the patterns in observations, blood results and other parts of the record that came before that happened. When it finds that pattern, it asks for a clinical review.

Amara is part way through her afternoon when an alert appears on the screen in front of her. It is about Mr Okafor.

She has seen Mr Okafor twice today. He is 71, was admitted four days ago and, by every account, is getting better. At lunch he was sitting out in the chair, finishing his soup and complaining about the football. His last observations, taken two hours ago, looked within his usual range to her.

The alert does not say what it noticed. It gives a risk level, a time window and a request.

Amara reads it twice. It does not fit the man she saw at lunch. She also knows that she saw him two hours ago, not now, and that the tool can draw on parts of the record she has not looked at today.

Two views of one patient

What Amara knows, and what the tool shows

What Amara knows

  • At lunch he was sitting out, eating and chatting.
  • His observations two hours ago looked within his usual range.
  • Her impression is that he is improving.
  • Her picture is two hours old.

What the tool shows

  • A risk level: elevated.
  • A prediction window: deterioration within the next 12 hours.
  • A request: clinical review.
  • It does not show the information that triggered the alert.

Neither view is complete. The tool cannot see Mr Okafor finishing his soup. Amara cannot see everything the tool weighed, and she has not seen him since lunch.

Response A

Pass it straight on

What Amara does. She sends the alert to a senior colleague with a short message: "The deterioration tool has flagged Mr Okafor, can you review?" Then she returns to her afternoon.

What happens

Her colleague reviews Mr Okafor later in the afternoon and finds nothing new. Over the next 12 hours he stays stable and needs no additional treatment.

What this response gets right

Escalating is never wrong in itself. The tool asked for a review, and asking a colleague for help is always allowed. The alert did not go unanswered.

What it misses

Nothing went with the escalation except the tool's output. Amara knew things the tool did not, such as how he looked at lunch, and she did not pass them on or check them again. Her colleague received a risk level with no clinical picture.

Here the tool made the call and the people carried it out. Giving a machine's output more weight than it has earned, and letting it stand in for your own look, is called automation bias. This time it cost a colleague's time. On a busier shift, alerts passed on unexamined compete with patients who need that colleague more.

Response B

Dismiss it

What Amara does. She acknowledges the alert, closes it and carries on. She does not see Mr Okafor again before the next planned observations, and she does not write anything about the alert.

What happens

Over the next 12 hours Mr Okafor stays stable and needs no additional treatment. Looking back, Amara's sense that he was well matched what happened.

What this response gets right

Amara trusted her own clinical picture, and that picture was reasonable. Alert fatigue is real: when a tool raises many alarms, people stop listening. Her doubt about the alert was worth having.

What it misses

At 14:10 Amara could not know how the next 12 hours would go. Her picture was two hours old, and the tool may have been weighing results she had not seen. The good outcome does not show that dismissing was safe. It shows that, this time, nothing happened.

Closing the alert without a note also leaves no record of why. Nobody can learn from it, including the people who check how the tool is performing. If Amara thinks the tool raises too many false alarms, the route is to report that, not to quietly stop listening to it.

Response C

Review, then decide

What Amara does. She goes to see Mr Okafor. Within what her role covers, she asks how he is feeling, checks him now rather than relying on lunchtime, and looks at what has been added to his record since the morning. She compares all of this with her earlier picture. Nothing she finds worries her. She writes down the alert, what she checked and what she found, tells the person in charge of the shift as her local process asks, and agrees when he will next be checked.

What happens

Over the next 12 hours Mr Okafor stays stable and needs no additional treatment.

What this response gets right

It does what the alert asked, and adds the one thing the tool cannot provide: a fresh look at the actual patient. It would also have been the right response if Mr Okafor had become unwell. That is what makes it a sound decision rather than a lucky one.

Her written review turns a single alert into useful information: a flag, a check and an outcome that the team monitoring the tool can count.

What it costs

It takes time out of a busy afternoon. If many alerts turn out like this one, every review has a cost. How often a tool flags patients who stay well, and whether that is acceptable, are questions for the rest of this week.

Debrief

Same ending, three decisions

All three responses end the same way: Mr Okafor stays stable for 12 hours and needs no additional treatment. That is deliberate. If you judged each response by its ending, they would all look equally good.

They are not equally good. A decision can only use what was known when it was made. At 14:10, nobody knew how the next 12 hours would go. Response C is the one that would have held up whichever way things went.

What the outcome tells us about the alert

We can say

  • The tool predicted deterioration within 12 hours.
  • Mr Okafor did not deteriorate within those 12 hours.
  • For this window, the alert was a false positive: it flagged a problem that did not happen.

We cannot say

  • Why the tool flagged him. The outcome alone does not tell us.
  • That any response prevented deterioration. No additional treatment was given.
  • Whether the tool is good or bad overall. One case cannot show that.
  • Whether the next alert, for the next patient, will be wrong.

Every early-warning tool produces some false positives. One false positive tells you very little. The questions that matter are how often it happens, for which patients, and at what cost.
